# Loathing in the Woes of Ruthlessness
## The Chosen One

**A novel**

### Prologue: The Name Beneath the Ash

Before the city learned to fear the dark, it learned to worship the light.

Every tower in Veyr carried a white flame at its summit. The flames were said to be blessings from the First Crown, proof that the heavens had chosen the city above all others. They burned through rain, through winter, through the long black seasons when the sun disappeared behind the ash clouds.

But the flames were fed by something.

Mara learned that on the night her mother died.

She was thirteen, barefoot in the snow, standing outside the Hall of Names while the city bells counted the dead. Her mother’s name had been carved into the black ledger of the Ruthless: people who had refused the Crown’s commands, people who had asked too many questions, people who had chosen mercy when obedience would have been safer.

Mara’s mother had been a healer. She had given medicine to a village marked for silence. The Crown called it treason. Mara called it love.

Before the guards dragged the healer away, she pressed a small iron key into her daughter’s palm.

“Do not become what they hate,” she whispered.

“What do they hate?” Mara asked.

Her mother looked toward the highest tower, where the white flame trembled against the clouds.

“Someone who remembers.”

Then the doors closed.

Years later, Mara would understand that the key did not open a door. It opened a truth.

### Chapter One: The Chosen Mark

At twenty-seven, Mara Vale had forgotten how to pray.

She still knew the motions. Kneel. Lower the head. Touch two fingers to the lips and one to the heart. Speak the Crown’s vow: *Order is mercy. Obedience is peace. The chosen are protected.*

She simply no longer believed the words.

Her work was to copy names from old records into new ones. Veyr had become very efficient at erasing people. A prisoner vanished from the Hall of Names, and Mara replaced the ink with a clean line. A family was expelled beyond the wall, and she removed their address. A district was declared contaminated, and she stamped every citizen within it as uncounted.

The work was called preservation.

Mara called it disappearance.

On the morning the mark appeared, she was copying the names of children from the eastern district. Each name had been crossed out in red. Their crime was being born in a district where the Crown’s soldiers had failed to collect the required grain.

Mara dipped her pen into the ink.

A pain opened beneath her ribs.

She dropped the pen. A silver line spread across the skin of her left hand, forming a circle split by a narrow vertical stroke. The mark burned once, then cooled.

Every clerk in the room fell silent.

The oldest among them, a woman named Sera, whispered, “The Chosen.”

The captain arrived before the ink dried. He wore a mask of polished bone and carried the red cord of the Crown’s hunters.

“Mara Vale,” he said. “You will come with us.”

She looked down at the children’s names.

“Where?”

“To the Crown.”

“And if I refuse?”

The captain tilted his head. “Then the mark will decide what you are.”

Mara thought of her mother’s final words. Do not become what they hate.

She closed her marked hand.

“Then let it decide.”

### Chapter Two: The House of Obedience

The Crown did not sit on a throne.

It lived in a room beneath the city, behind seven gates, inside a chamber lined with mirrors. No one knew whether the ruler was a person, a council, or a machine built by the founders. The people called it the Sovereign because fear preferred a single face.

Mara was led through the gates by Captain Orin Thane, the same man who had stood outside the Hall of Names on the night her mother disappeared.

He had changed less than she expected. His hair was gray at the temples, but his eyes were still the color of wet stone.

“You remember me,” he said.

“I remember everyone who carries a red cord.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is enough.”

He stopped at the seventh gate. “The mark does not make you noble.”

“I never said it did.”

“It makes you useful.”

“Useful to whom?”

“To whoever reaches you first.”

The gate opened.

Inside the chamber, hundreds of mirrors reflected Mara from every direction. In one, she was a child. In another, she was old. In a third, she wore the red cord. In the farthest mirror, her mother stood beside her.

Mara turned sharply.

The reflection did not.

Her mother’s image raised one hand and pointed toward the floor.

A seam ran through the stone beneath Mara’s feet.

The Sovereign spoke from everywhere at once.

“Marked one. You have been selected.”

“By whom?”

“By necessity.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only answer that survives.”

The mirrors darkened. Images appeared: villages burned by rebels, children starving beyond the wall, soldiers refusing orders, citizens tearing down white flames. Then came an image of the city without the Crown. The towers collapsed. The rivers ran black. People turned upon one another in the streets.

“Ruthlessness is the cost of peace,” said the Sovereign.

Mara stared at the burning city.

“And who pays the cost?”

“All who are protected.”

The mark on her hand began to glow.

Captain Thane leaned close. “Do not make the mistake of thinking cruelty is strength. Cruelty is often only fear with an army.”

Mara looked at him. “You sound almost sorry.”

“I sound tired.”

“Then stop serving it.”

For the first time, the captain looked away.

### Chapter Three: The Woes of Ruthlessness

The rebels lived beneath the old aqueduct, where the city’s waste water passed through tunnels older than the Crown. They had no banners and no single leader. Some wanted justice. Some wanted revenge. Some wanted only to see the white flames extinguished.

Their leader was a boy named Iven, sixteen years old and missing two fingers on his right hand.

“You are late,” he told Mara.

“I was escorted.”

“By Thane?”

“Yes.”

“Then you are either dangerous or doomed.”

“Those are not opposites.”

Iven studied the mark on her hand. “The stories say the Chosen can open the First Gate.”

“The stories also say the Chosen will save everyone.”

“Will you?”

“No.”

He smiled without humor. “Good. I distrust saviors.”

The rebels showed her the truth hidden below the city. The white flames were not blessings. They were lamps powered by a network of ancient engines. The engines drew heat from the ground and memory from the people. Every erased name strengthened the Crown. Every forgotten crime fed the flames.

The city had not been protected from darkness.

It had been taught to forget it.

Mara found her mother’s name in a chamber beneath the aqueduct. It was not crossed out. It had been moved into a column titled *Necessary Losses*.

Beside it was a note in her mother’s handwriting:

*The Crown cannot be defeated by hatred. Hatred is the language it taught us first.*

Mara read the words until they blurred.

Iven stood behind her. “Your mother believed in mercy.”

“She believed mercy had to be defended.”

“Is that different?”

“It is harder.”

The rebels planned to seize the central engine during the Festival of First Light. They would extinguish the highest flame, expose the Crown’s records, and call the city to choose its own future.

Mara saw the danger immediately.

“If you reveal everything at once, people will panic.”

“They are already afraid.”

“Fear does not make people free. Sometimes it makes them cruel.”

Iven tightened his jaw. “Then what do you suggest?”

“Give them the truth before you ask them to carry it.”

No one liked the plan.

That was how Mara knew it might work.

### Chapter Four: The Captain’s Confession

Captain Thane found Mara alone beside the underground river.

“You should leave,” he said.

“From the rebels?”

“From the city.”

“I have tried leaving.”

“No. You have tried surviving.”

He removed the red cord from his uniform and placed it on the stone between them.

“My son was marked twelve years ago,” he said. “He was nine.”

Mara said nothing.

“The Sovereign said he was dangerous. I believed them. I signed the order.”

The river carried a thin skin of reflected light through the darkness.

“What happened?”

“I took him to the Hall of Names myself.”

Mara’s hands tightened.

“I thought the Crown would question him. I thought they would frighten him and release him. Instead, they erased him. Every record. Every memory in the city. Even his mother forgot his face.”

“And you?”

“I remembered because I had signed the order.”

He looked older than he had that morning.

“Why tell me?”

“Because the Crown has chosen you for the same reason it chose him. You remember what others accept.”

“You served it.”

“Yes.”

“You helped erase people.”

“Yes.”

“You expect forgiveness?”

“No. I expect consequences.”

Mara picked up the red cord and threw it into the river.

“Then face them with us.”

Thane watched the cord disappear.

“I am not good enough to lead a revolution.”

“Neither am I.”

“Then why do you keep going?”

Mara looked toward the hidden chamber where her mother’s name waited beneath the city.

“Because goodness is not a feeling. It is a direction.”

### Chapter Five: The Festival of First Light

The festival began at midnight.

Every citizen gathered beneath the towers. The Crown distributed white cloth, sweet bread, and masks painted with smiling mouths. Children carried paper flames. Priests sang the old vow.

Mara walked among them wearing no mask.

Iven’s rebels were scattered through the crowd. Thane stood on the western platform beside the soldiers. The captain’s uniform was restored, but the red cord was gone.

At the stroke of the first bell, the highest tower opened.

The white flame rose like a second sun.

Then the mirrors appeared across the city.

They hung in the air above the square, each one showing a name, a face, a sentence, and a choice. The records of the Crown spread across the night. People saw their missing parents. Their erased children. Their vanished neighbors. They saw who had ordered the disappearances and who had signed the orders.

The city began to scream.

The Sovereign spoke through the bells.

“Observe the result of truth without order.”

The crowd turned upon itself. Some shouted for revenge. Some tore down the paper flames. Soldiers raised their weapons.

Mara climbed the central steps.

“Do not let them choose your anger for you!” she shouted.

No one heard her.

A stone struck her shoulder. A soldier fired into the air. Somewhere, a child began to cry.

The Crown had predicted this. It had always known that truth could become another weapon.

Mara looked at the mark on her hand.

The First Gate was beneath the highest tower.

The key her mother had given her fit the lock.

She could open the gate and destroy the engine. The white flames would die. The city would lose its protection, its records, its power, and perhaps its life.

Or she could leave the engine intact and become the next voice of the Crown.

The Sovereign whispered inside her mind.

“Choose ruthlessness. Become strong enough to save them.”

Mara remembered her mother’s note.

Hatred is the language it taught us first.

She turned the key.

### Chapter Six: The Chosen Choice

The First Gate opened onto a chamber of roots and fire.

At its center stood the engine: a black sphere suspended above a basin of human names. Every name erased by the Crown drifted inside the sphere like a spark. The engine did not burn memory.

It trapped it.

Mara stepped toward the basin. The voices of the forgotten rose around her.

Her mother was among them.

“Do not destroy it,” the voice said.

Mara stopped.

“Why?”

“Because destruction is easy when you are angry.”

“What should I do?”

“Return what was taken.”

The engine had three levers. One controlled the flames. One controlled the records. One controlled the Crown’s voice.

The third lever was guarded by the Sovereign’s final defense: a figure formed from black glass. It wore Mara’s face.

“I am what you become if you win,” it said.

“You are what I become if I surrender.”

“Both are victory.”

The glass figure attacked.

Mara fought without a sword. She fought with the iron key, with the memory of her mother’s hands, with the knowledge that the figure wanted her to answer violence with violence. Each time she struck, it returned stronger.

So she stopped striking.

The figure drove a glass blade toward her heart. Mara caught the blade in both hands. It cut her palms. She did not release it.

“I will not hate you,” she said.

The figure cracked.

“I will not become you.”

The figure shattered.

Mara pulled the second lever.

The names rose from the sphere and poured into the city.

Every erased person became remembered. Every hidden record appeared before the people who had lost it. The Crown’s voice broke into a thousand separate voices, and then into silence.

The white flames went dark.

For one breath, Veyr stood beneath the stars.

Then the engines began to fail.

### Chapter Seven: After the Flame

The city did not become free in a single night.

Freedom was not a door. It was a room people had to learn how to build together.

The first week was terrible. There were trials, riots, thefts, and grief. Some citizens wanted the old order restored because order was easier than responsibility. Some rebels wanted every former Crown official executed. Some soldiers refused to lay down their weapons.

Mara spoke at the Hall of Names.

“Justice cannot mean becoming the Crown with different uniforms,” she told the crowd. “If we build a new cruelty from the ruins of the old one, then the old one has won.”

Many hated her for saying it.

That was acceptable.

Iven organized food distribution. Thane stood trial for his role in the erasures. He did not ask Mara to defend him. He testified against the Crown and accepted the sentence the city’s new council gave him: years of service beyond the wall, rebuilding the villages his orders had harmed.

Mara kept the iron key.

The engines were not destroyed. They were dismantled, piece by piece, and their heat redirected to hospitals, schools, and winter shelters. The white flames were replaced by smaller lamps that anyone could light and anyone could extinguish.

No light was called eternal again.

Months after the festival, a child approached Mara outside the Hall of Names.

“Are you the Chosen One?” the child asked.

Mara looked at the mark on her hand. It had faded to a thin silver scar.

“No,” she said. “I was chosen once.”

“What is the difference?”

“Being chosen is something that happens to you. Choosing is something you do.”

The child considered this.

“Then what did you choose?”

Mara looked at the city. It was wounded, noisy, uncertain, and alive.

“To remember without becoming cruel.”

### Epilogue: The Lamp Anyone Can Carry

Years later, the Hall of Names became a library.

Its black ledgers remained, but no one was erased from them. Names were added, corrected, questioned, and sometimes forgiven. The city learned that remembrance was not the same as worship and that forgiveness did not cancel consequence.

Mara never became queen.

She refused every crown offered to her.

Instead, she traveled beyond the wall with healers, teachers, and engineers. She visited the villages whose names had been removed. She listened to the people who did not trust Veyr. She helped build roads that went in both directions.

On the anniversary of the First Light, the city gathered without masks.

Each person carried a small lamp.

There was no central flame. No voice from beneath the earth. No chosen ruler promising peace in exchange for obedience.

Only thousands of separate lights, trembling in human hands.

Mara stood at the edge of the square with the iron key around her neck.

A young woman beside her asked, “Do you ever miss the certainty?”

Mara watched the lamps rise.

“Every day.”

“Then why give it up?”

“Because certainty made us ruthless.”

“And uncertainty?”

“Uncertainty makes us responsible.”

The bells began to ring.

Not to command.

To remember.

Mara lifted her lamp, and the people lifted theirs. The city glowed without a single chosen flame.

For the first time, the darkness did not look like an enemy.

It looked like space.

Space for truth.

Space for mercy.

Space for the next choice.

**The End**
